Methodology

How the grades work, where every number comes from, and, just as important, what this data can’t tell you. If you find an error, the corrections policy is at the bottom. Errors get fixed and disclosed, not buried.

The question and the theory behind it

The scorecard grades one question: is Spokane attracting employers that raise wages?

The theory is old, boring, and well supported. A regional economy grows when its employers sell to the outside world. That means manufacturers, software firms, engineering and professional services, and health systems treating patients from out of the area. Those sales bring in money that was not here before. Local-serving businesses matter, but they mostly move around money that is already here.

So when this page says “good-fit employers,” it means employers in traded sectors who pay above the regional average and hire the skills Spokane already has. A traded sector is one that sells outside the region. The eight measures follow that chain. Jobs. Who is creating them. What they pay. What households have left to spend. And whether that spending reaches storefronts.

How grades are assigned

Green, shown on the scorecard as Strong, means Spokane beats its benchmark (usually the U.S. or Washington state) or is clearly improving. Yellow, shown as Mixed, means near the benchmark, or the signals point in different directions. Red, shown as Weak, means below benchmark and not improving. The color names and the plain-language names are the same three grades. Earlier change log entries use the color names.

The overall call. The top of the scorecard reports one verdict for the region: off track, mixed, or on track. The rule is fixed before each reading and applied mechanically. Off track when weak measures outnumber strong ones. Mixed when they tie. On track when strong measures outnumber weak ones. Yellow and mixed measures never decide the call on their own; they are the middle of the board, not a tiebreaker.

There is no letter grade and no combined score, on purpose. The eight measures are not equally important. They are not independent of each other either, since spending and storefronts move partly because jobs move. Averaging them would suggest a precision this data does not have. A three-state verdict gives a reader the headline without inventing math to justify it.

Grades are judgment calls, and pretending otherwise would be dishonest. A formula would look more objective while hiding the same judgments inside weightings nobody reads. Instead, every grade is argued in one paragraph, in public, next to its source. If you think a grade is wrong, the evidence to argue with is right there. That’s the point.

One rule keeps grades honest over time: a grade only changes when the data changes, not when the news cycle does. Each quarterly update notes every grade change and why.

A second rule keeps the record honest. Before any figure is updated, the old edition is frozen into the public archive, exactly as it was published. Archived editions are never edited. If one of them turns out to be wrong, the correction shows up as a new entry in the change log below, and the original stays visible. A scorecard that can rewrite its own past is not a scorecard.

What this data can’t tell you

Read the scorecard with these six limits in mind. They’re why some claims you might want to make from this page shouldn’t be made.

  • It measures results, not blame. Regional results reflect national interest rates, state policy, Greater Spokane Inc., the county, and luck, on top of anything city hall does. A weak grade means the region is losing ground. It does not prove any one official caused it. The scorecard’s claim is narrower: these are the numbers leadership should be judged against and organized around.
  • The data is old the day it’s published. Most figures lag three months to a year, and federal disruptions make it worse. BLS canceled an entire month of unemployment publications during the last shutdown. A grade describes the most recent measured period, not this morning.
  • Numbers get revised. BLS and Census routinely revise earlier estimates, sometimes by a lot. When a revision changes a grade, the update will say so rather than quietly repainting history.
  • Surveys have error bars. The county’s 2024 median household income of $86,206 carries a margin of error around $3,400, and it jumped $12,000+ in a single year for reasons economists can’t yet fully explain. Single-year jumps in survey data earn skepticism here, even when they’re good news. That grade stays provisional until a second year confirms it.
  • Averages hide geography. County figures blend downtown, the Valley, and everything between. Retail vacancy is “healthy” in Spokane Valley and weak downtown, and a metro average shows neither. Where a split matters, the scorecard says so in the text.
  • A good number can have a bad cause. Spokane’s unemployment rate beats the state and the nation partly because retirements are shrinking the labor force, not because hiring is strong. Denominators matter, and each grade’s paragraph flags this kind of thing when it applies.

Two sources deserve a specific disclosure. Vacancy data comes from CoStar. Sector data comes from Lightcast. Both are private datasets that local groups republish. Neither can be checked against raw public records without paying for a subscription, so both are labeled wherever they appear.

About the employer calculator

The calculator is a teaching tool, not a forecast. It shows the local multiplier at work. That is the well-documented finding that jobs selling to the outside world create extra local jobs, while jobs serving local demand mostly move around money that is already here. It shows ranges, never single numbers, and it cites where the ranges come from.

Independence and corrections

This scorecard is kept by one Spokane resident, on personal time. It takes no money from anyone. It is not tied to the City of Spokane, any campaign, party, developer, or business group. It exists for one reason: to keep attention on the numbers that decide whether this region’s neighborhoods thrive. Background, possible biases and the corrections policy are on the who made this page.

Corrections: if a number is wrong, send the source. Verified errors are fixed within a week and logged below, permanently. Disagreements about grades are welcome too, and the strongest counterarguments will be linked so readers can weigh them.
